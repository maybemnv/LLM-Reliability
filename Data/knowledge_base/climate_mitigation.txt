Climate Change Mitigation: Strategies and Technologies

Source: International Energy Agency Report 2023

Introduction

Climate change mitigation refers to efforts to reduce or prevent greenhouse gas emissions to limit the magnitude of global warming. Achieving the goals of the Paris Agreement requires transformative changes across energy systems, industry, transportation, buildings, and land use.

Energy Sector Transformation

Renewable Energy

Solar Power: Solar photovoltaic (PV) costs have declined by approximately 90% since 2010, making it the cheapest source of new electricity generation in most regions. Utility-scale solar capacity is expanding rapidly worldwide.

Wind Power: Both onshore and offshore wind power have become cost-competitive with fossil fuels. Offshore wind offers higher capacity factors and access to stronger, more consistent winds.

Hydroelectric Power: Hydropower remains the largest source of renewable electricity globally. Pumped hydro storage provides valuable grid balancing services.

Nuclear Energy: Nuclear power provides low-carbon baseload electricity. Small modular reactors (SMRs) may address cost and safety concerns of traditional nuclear plants.

Energy Storage

Battery technology, particularly lithium-ion batteries, has improved dramatically in cost and performance. Grid-scale storage is increasingly viable for balancing variable renewable generation. Emerging technologies include:

- Flow batteries for long-duration storage
- Compressed air energy storage
- Green hydrogen production and storage
- Thermal energy storage systems

Transportation Decarbonization

Electric Vehicles

Battery electric vehicles (BEVs) are rapidly gaining market share. Key developments include:

- Declining battery costs approaching $100/kWh
- Extended driving ranges exceeding 400 km for many models
- Expanding charging infrastructure
- Heavy-duty electric trucks entering the market

Sustainable Fuels

For applications difficult to electrify, sustainable fuels offer alternatives:

- Green hydrogen produced from renewable electricity
- Sustainable aviation fuels from biomass or synthetic processes
- Ammonia as a marine fuel
- Biofuels from sustainable feedstocks

Industrial Decarbonization

Heavy industries face particular challenges in reducing emissions:

Steel Production: Green hydrogen-based direct reduced iron (DRI) can replace coal-based processes. Electric arc furnaces using scrap and clean electricity offer near-term pathways.

Cement: Carbon capture and storage (CCS) may be necessary for cement production. Alternative materials and improved efficiency can reduce demand.

Chemicals: Electrification, hydrogen feedstocks, and bio-based processes can reduce petrochemical emissions.

Carbon Capture and Storage

CCS technologies capture CO2 from industrial processes or directly from the atmosphere for permanent storage or utilization:

- Point-source capture from power plants and industrial facilities
- Direct air capture (DAC) removes CO2 directly from ambient air
- Carbon storage in geological formations
- Carbon utilization in building materials and fuels

Policy Mechanisms

Effective policies to drive mitigation include:

1. Carbon Pricing: Carbon taxes or emissions trading systems create financial incentives for emission reductions.

2. Regulations: Standards for vehicles, appliances, and buildings mandate efficiency improvements.

3. Subsidies: Financial support for clean technologies accelerates deployment and cost reduction.

4. Public Investment: Government funding for research, development, and infrastructure.

5. International Cooperation: Technology transfer and financial support for developing countries.

Conclusion

The technologies and strategies needed for climate mitigation are largely available. The challenge lies in accelerating their deployment through supportive policies, adequate investment, and international cooperation. Early action reduces costs and risks compared to delayed mitigation efforts.
